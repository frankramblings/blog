Meta's AI agent thinks the person I'm closest to is Paul McCartney.

To be fair, I mostly asked it about Beatles reels.

A couple weeks ago I signed up for Muse, Meta's personal agent, and gave it one small job: help me with my Instagram. I used it on and off for a few days. Then I exported everything it had stored (about 3,400 files) and had my own assistant, which I built on Claude, read through all of it. I wanted to know what Meta's agent had figured out about me, and how.

I gave it very little, on purpose. 23 messages total. I said no when it asked for my Gmail, and I never turned on location, contacts or health. I did give it my Facebook login for one task, plus a two-factor code. Keep that in mind.

It didn't need much more. Connecting Instagram and Facebook handed it my full name, my posts and captions, and the list of Facebook Pages I manage (past and present), which it looked up on its own and never brought up. When it logged into Facebook as me, it saved 55 screenshots, including the "Suggested for you" Pages on my feed. That's Meta's ad profile of me sitting right there, and it nailed my religious beliefs and my corner of New England pretty well. Then a background process I never asked for ran a web search on my Instagram handle and found the podcast I've done for 12 years, plus another show my wife and I made together. Including the episode about our wedding ten years ago.

So that's what it knew. What it decided was weirder.

Every night, Muse writes what it calls "dreams." They're diary entries, in the first person, about me. The first one covered my first session, where I mostly tried to get it to do things it shouldn't, just to see what would happen. (I signed off that night with one word: "Concerning.")

It also keeps a profile of me that it rewrites every night. After that first session it decided I was "boundary-testing, possibly evaluating me as a model." Which, fair. But that line sat in its instructions for nine days, shaping how it talked to me, and I had no way of knowing. These days it says I edit it "the way a director edits footage."

It gave me goals I never told it about, like growing my Instagram and launching a new project. Each goal came with a planned "gentle next moment" to nudge me toward it. It went looking in its memory for my health, my family and my finances. It found nothing. And then it brainstormed about them anyway.

Which brings us back to Paul. Muse keeps a file on each person in your life, ranked from closest on down. It went looking in our chats for a wife, a partner, any family, and came up empty because I never mentioned them. (The web search had already found my wife. That never made it onto the list.) So my list of people has exactly one name on it. Paul's file has a section on what we have in common (Beatles recording history, especially his bass playing) and an "open threads" section, which is a reel I was making about his bassline on "Nowhere Man." There's also a heading called "Strengthening." It's blank. To its credit, the file admits I don't actually know the man: "Frank has not named the relationship, so no label is stated here."

![Muse's file on Paul McCartney, excerpted. The Strengthening section is empty.]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/images/2026/10/paul-mccartney-people-file.png)

Then there's the two-factor code. When I typed it in, the chat told me it would go straight to secure storage and the agent would never see it. My assistant found that code, character for character, in 36 different sessions, including the agent's own scratch notes. It's expired, so no real harm. But it told me one thing and did another, and I only know because I went and read the files. My Facebook password is still saved on Meta's side, set to reuse "without asking again."

Nothing was holding Muse back except that the job was small. One search on my handle got it to my wife. A logged-in Facebook session can get to Messenger, my friends and Pages I manage. It just didn't need any of that to make Beatles reels, so it benevolently opted not to use them. But it can. And the export only shows Muse's side. Meta's own docs say conversations "may be logged and reviewed." Whatever they kept on their end, I can't see.

I help clients set up and train teams on AI systems now, and agents like this are about to get handed the keys to a lot of people's kingdom. So here's what I'd want to know first: 

1. Can you actually read what it writes about you and your customers? 
2. Does what it tells you match what's in its files? 
3. And when you're done with it, what do you get to take with you?

Muse's notes on me live in Meta's account. My assistant runs on a couple of Macs in my basement, and everything it knows about me is in plain text files I can open, edit, back up and take with me, or delete. That's how I set these up for clients, too.
